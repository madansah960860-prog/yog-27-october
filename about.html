About Thistledown Kitchen

A seasonal recipe journal published from Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. We write recipes, test them until they work for someone who was not standing next to us, and sell printable cards and a digital cookbook.

Why the year, and not the cuisine

Most recipe sites are organized by course or by cuisine, and that works perfectly well if you already know what you want to cook. It works badly if what you actually have is four pounds of apples and a Tuesday evening. Organizing by season solves the second problem, which in a home kitchen is the more common one.

It also keeps the writing honest. A recipe written for the ingredient at its best in September is a different recipe from the same dish attempted in March with imported fruit, and pretending otherwise is how people end up disappointed in themselves rather than in the strawberries. Where a recipe depends on the ingredient being in season, we say so plainly, and where it does not, we say that too.

How a recipe gets onto this site

There are three passes, and a recipe does not get published until it has been through all of them.

  1. The first cook. Somebody makes the thing, usually because there is a surplus of something that needs using. Quantities are approximate and nothing is written down except in a notebook on the counter.
  2. The written version. The dish is made again, this time weighing and measuring as we go, with timings taken from a clock rather than from memory. This is where the oven temperature gets pinned down and where we find out that the thing we called "a handful" is actually about a cup and a half.
  3. The cold test. Somebody who was not present for either of the first two cooks makes the recipe from the written page, in their own kitchen, with their own oven, and is asked to follow it exactly even where it seems wrong. Every point where they hesitated becomes an edit.

The third pass is the one that matters. It is where "cook until done" turns into "cook until the edges pull away from the pan and the middle no longer wobbles when you shake it", and where we discover that a step we thought was obvious was not obvious at all. Most of the boxes you will see on the recipe pages — the ones headed Make it easier — came straight out of a cold test.

Our editorial standards

These are the rules we hold ourselves to, written down so you can hold us to them as well.

  • Every recipe is tested. If it has not been through all three passes above, it is not published.
  • Quantities are in US cups, tablespoons, ounces and pounds, with oven temperatures in degrees Fahrenheit, because that is what the kitchens we write for use.
  • Nutrition figures are estimates and are labelled as estimates. They are calculated from standard ingredient data, not measured in a laboratory, and they will shift with brands and portion size. They are not dietary advice.
  • Allergens are listed on every recipe. We name the major allergens present in the ingredients as written, and we say plainly that you need to check the labels on what you actually buy, because manufacturers change formulations without notice.
  • We do not make health claims. This is a cooking site. We will tell you a soup is good on a cold evening. We will not tell you what it does for your body, because we are not qualified to and nobody should be taking that kind of advice from a recipe page.
  • No testimonials, no star ratings, no reviews. We do not publish them anywhere on this site. There is no way for a reader to tell a real one from an invented one, so we leave the whole category alone.
  • No affiliate links. When we name a piece of equipment we are not being paid to name it, and there is no commission attached. If that ever changes, the disclosure will appear directly beside the link, not in the footer.
  • Corrections are made in the open. If we get something wrong, we fix the page and add a dated line at the bottom saying what changed. We do not quietly edit and move on.

What we sell, and why

Two things, both digital, both charged once.

The first is a set of printable recipe cards — four sets of 12 cards, one per season, at $9.00 a set. They exist because a laptop on a kitchen counter next to a mixing bowl is a bad idea, and because a card you can wipe clean and put back in a box is still the most practical way to cook from a recipe.

The second is A Thistledown Year, a 148-page digital cookbook at $19.00. It collects 64 recipes in season order with a month-by-month buying guide and a chapter on putting food away. It is a PDF. There is no app and no account.

Both are one-time purchases. Neither renews. Neither requires a subscription. If something you bought is not right for you, the refund window is 14 days and you do not have to explain yourself.

How the site pays for itself

Two ways, and you should know about both. The first is the cards and the book above. The second is advertising: this site carries third-party display advertising, and the slots are labelled Advertisement so you can tell at a glance what is ours and what is not. Advertisers have no say in what we publish and no advance sight of it. There is more detail on the Advertising and Affiliate Disclosure page.

Getting in touch

If a recipe did not work, we would genuinely rather hear about it than not. Tell us which recipe, what you did, and what happened, and we will either help you fix it or fix the page. Write to hello@thistledownkitchen.com or use the contact form. We answer on weekdays, usually within two business days.
